
<Statement label="The goal">

How could I make it easier for customers to find a BBQ restaurant, build an order, and check out within a single app?

</Statement>

<Section label="Context">

BarbeQueue was a mobile ordering concept I designed as a solo UX course project over two months.

The idea was to bring BBQ restaurant discovery and ordering into one place, supporting both dine-in/pre-queuing and catering orders.

I explored the initial ordering experience through wireframing, prototyping, usability testing, and one round of design revisions.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/KeyScreens.png" alt="BarbeQueue application concept" caption="BarbeQueue — a mobile BBQ ordering concept" aspect="16/9" />

</Section>

<Section label="Testing the first ordering flow">

I started with the assumption that customers would benefit from being able to browse restaurants and prepare orders without switching between individual restaurant apps.

To explore how that might work, I mapped out the ordering journey and built a low-fidelity prototype in Figma.

I then conducted a remote usability study with **7 participants**, asking them to work through restaurant selection, food ordering, and checkout.

I wanted to understand not just whether participants could navigate the screens, but where the flow stopped matching their expectations.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/LofiPrototypeFlow.png" alt="Initial low-fidelity ordering flow" caption="The initial prototype used during usability testing" aspect="16/9" />

</Section>

<Section label="What I learned and changed">

The study surfaced several gaps between the flow I had designed and how participants expected to place an order.

Three findings shaped the next iteration.

### 01 — Ordering more than one item was frustrating

> "This is annoying, I'm not here just to order 1 satay only."

One participant struggled to return to the menu after selecting an item. The flow had been designed around individual selections rather than building a complete order.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/WireframeParticipantInsight2.png" alt="Revised ordering experience with cart" caption="The original ordering flow in the Lofi Prototype" aspect="16/9" />

**4 of 7 participants** wanted to order multiple menu items, and 3 of those participants also wanted a cart to save their selections. By placing the order automatically, it meant users couldn't have a chance to reverse on what they had selected initially, or they could not stack up multiple orders before carting out.

**What I changed:** I revised the ordering experience to include an updatable cart where customers could add multiple items and review their selections before checkout.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/RevisionBuildingOrder.png" alt="Revised ordering experience with cart" caption="Revising the flow to support multi-item orders" aspect="16/9" />

### 02 — Returning customers wanted a faster route to ordering

**5 of 7 participants** expressed a preference for completing their order in under five minutes, while **4 of 7** wanted the ability to favourite restaurants.

One participant also questioned why they couldn't save their previous order for next time.

This suggested that repeat customers shouldn't need to start from restaurant discovery every time.

**What I changed:** I lessened the whitespace on the home screen and added a way for customers to save their favourite restaurants, while making it accessible from their profile.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/RevisionSavedRestaurants.png" alt="Saved restaurant feature" caption="Giving returning customers easier access to favourite restaurants" aspect="16/9" />

### 03 — Checkout needed more payment flexibility

**6 of 7 participants** wanted the option to pay cash on delivery.

The initial checkout experience did not sufficiently account for this preference.

**What I changed:** I revised the checkout design to make cash on delivery an explicit payment option.

<Figure src="/images/work/2023-05-barbequeue-google-ux-certificate/assets/RevisionCheckout.png" alt="Revised payment selection" caption="Making cash on delivery visible during checkout" aspect="16/9" />

</Section>

<Section label="Outcome">

The revised concept responded to three recurring needs from testing: building multi-item orders, returning to favourite restaurants, and having more choice at checkout.

The project concluded after one usability study and one round of revisions. I did not conduct a second test, so I couldn't verify whether these changes made the ordering experience faster or easier to complete.

</Section>

<Section label="Reflection">

One of the more useful lessons from this project was realising that individual screens could make sense on their own while the overall journey still felt frustrating.

I had designed a way to select food, but hadn't properly accounted for how someone would build an entire meal.

If I continued the project, I'd test the revised multi-item ordering flow and revisit the distinction between "Catering" and "Dine-In", which had also caused confusion during the first study.

BarbeQueue was my first experience connecting user feedback directly to design revisions. It changed how I thought about a prototype: not just as a way to present an idea, but as something that could reveal where my assumptions were wrong.

</Section>
