<Statement label="the challenge" tone="dark">
  How do you improve an existing enterprise <strong>product</strong> without disrupting the <strong>people, data and processes</strong> already relying on it?
</Statement>

<section className="editorial-section editorial-section--overview">

    <p className="editorial-eyebrow">01. Overview</p>
    <h2 className="type-title">Designing improvements within a live HR ecosystem.</h2>
    <p>At Accenture, I worked on change requests for an enterprise HR platform serving multiple public-sector organisations. Unlike an academic project, this was not a product I could design from scratch. Every improvement had to coexist with established workflows, existing records and different user groups.</p>
    <p>Two enhancements shaped how I think about product design: enabling reference checks to progress alongside another screening process, and ensuring users see only the job profiles relevant to their organisation.</p>
    <p><strong>My role:</strong> investigating requirements and existing behaviour, translating needs into functional solutions, coordinating with client and technical stakeholders, and designing positive, negative and regression test coverage.</p>

    <h2 className="type-title">The visible problem was rarely the whole problem.</h2>
    <p>Across both requests, I worked backwards from the experience users needed to the data and rules that made that experience possible. The goal was not only to deliver the immediate change, but to keep the solution understandable and maintainable for the teams inheriting it.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="research-visuals">
    <Figure
      src="/images/work/project-c-accenture/assets/DesignForDataPlaceholder.png"

      aspect="16/9"

  </div>
  <div className="editorial-section__intro flow">
    <h2 className="type-title">Designing for data meant thinking beyond delivery.</h2>
    <p>I looked for reusable logic rather than one-off fixes, considered how existing records would behave after a change, and made exceptions explicit so that future maintainers could understand why different users followed different paths.</p>
  </div>

Meanwhile, a related screening activity could take place independently. The opportunity was to make reference checks easier to initiate, track and complete without forcing the two activities into a single sequence.</p>

<section className="editorial-section editorial-section--research">
  <div className="editorial-section__intro flow">
    <p className="editorial-eyebrow">05. Feature two — job profile visibility</p>
    <h2 className="type-title">What looked like a dropdown problem was really a data ownership problem.</h2>
    <p>Users could encounter job profiles belonging to other organisations during certain HR tasks. Besides making selection harder, this created the risk of choosing an irrelevant profile. Simply hiding items on one screen would not address the underlying issue.</p>
    <p>The stronger solution was to establish which organisation owned each job profile, then use that relationship wherever the platform needed to determine visibility.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="research-visuals">
    <Figure
      src="/images/work/project-c-accenture/assets/JobProfileBeforeAfterPlaceholder.png"
      alt="Placeholder for a before and after job profile selection mockup with fictional agencies"
      caption="Visual placeholder — from mixed profiles to context-relevant choices; use fictional data."
      aspect="16/9"
    />
  </div>
  <div className="editorial-section__intro flow">
    <h2 className="type-title">One ownership model, multiple downstream experiences.</h2>
    <p>For newly created profiles, the organisation context could be captured during the request process. But existing profiles also needed to be mapped correctly. I analysed historical extracts, distinguished duplicate report rows from unique records, and helped prepare structured data for bulk updates.</p>
    <p>The design also needed to account for shared-service users supporting more than one organisation, and for processes where existing visibility was intentionally preserved.</p>
  </div>

<section className="editorial-section editorial-section--outcome">
  <div className="editorial-section__intro editorial-section__intro--storyboards flow">
    <p className="editorial-eyebrow">06. Outcome &amp; reflections</p>
    <h2 className="type-title">Designing for data made the changes more coherent.</h2>
    <p>Across both enhancements, my contribution was to connect a user-facing requirement to the underlying workflows, organisational rules, historical records and testing needed to support it. The reference-check work particularly challenged me to take ownership of an incomplete handover, navigate stakeholder frustration and collaborate closely with the client team under tight capacity constraints.</p>
    <p>This case study describes the solution design and delivery work I contributed to, not independently verified post-launch improvements. I do not have a reliable basis to claim measured reductions in processing time, errors or user effort.</p>
  </div>
  <div className="editorial-section__intro editorial-section__intro--storyboards flow">
    <h2 className="type-title">What I took away.</h2>
    <p>In academic projects, I often had the freedom to take an idea from zero to one. In enterprise work, I learned to start from what already existed: inherited decisions, imperfect documentation, live data, organisational constraints and people who needed answers quickly.</p>
    <p>One limitation of this work was that I could not conduct direct user research with agency users. The pain points and requirements came through the central client team, which meant I had to distinguish stakeholder-reported needs from behaviour I had personally observed. Given another iteration, I would prioritise direct observation and usability validation with the people carrying out these tasks.</p>
    <blockquote className="editorial-quote">
      <em>
        Good enterprise product design is not just what users see. It is how
        clearly the data, rules and exceptions behind that experience are
        designed for the next person who has to maintain them.
      </em>
    </blockquote>
